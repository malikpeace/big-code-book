Why engineers structure software this way, and what technical debt looks like

Architecture is two questions: where does this live, and what is it allowed to touch. Technical debt is what you owe when you answered those questions fast. Both are visible in your own repo, in numbers you can read today.

LAST TIME

Security is a boundary question: decide where you stop believing what you are told, then check size, identity, permission and rate on your side of that line. Performance is a measurement question: latency versus throughput, N+1, bundle size, caching, debouncing, and never optimise before you measure. Memento's proxy fails closed and its biggest CSS file is 411 KB.

Quick check

What does "fail closed" mean, and where does Memento do it?
When the check itself cannot answer, refuse. In supabase/functions/ai-proxy/index.ts:47-55, a rate limiter that cannot reach its counter returns a 503 instead of letting the call through.
A test fails. Is that bad news?
It is information, and usually good news, because the alternative was finding out from a user. A failing test is a spec that is currently not met. The bad case is a test that passes while the behaviour is wrong.
Why does index.html load Memento's scripts in a fixed numbered order?
They are classic scripts sharing one global scope, not modules. Nothing declares what it needs, so the order in the HTML is the only thing that guarantees a definition exists before it is used.

The idea

Take any working program and cut it into pieces. There are a thousand ways to cut it and they all run identically. They differ only in what happens the next time someone wants to change something. That is all architecture is: the arrangement that decides how expensive tomorrow is.

Two words carry most of the weight. Coupling is how much one piece has to know about another piece's insides. If changing a detail inside A forces an edit inside B, A and B are coupled. Cohesion is how much one file is about one thing. A file where everything belongs together is cohesive. A file that holds the card renderer, the share sheet, the grid layout and four helpers nobody could place elsewhere is not.

The aim is low coupling and high cohesion, and the reason is mechanical rather than aesthetic: you want the blast radius of a change to be small and predictable. A boundary is the deal you strike to get that. It says: here is what I promise, the rest is mine, and you may not reach past this line. Memento's native app has a boundary written as a single comment, src/clarity/aiPort.ts line 1: the Clarity screens know an interface, never a URL. Swap the transport and the screens do not move.

ANALOGY (TEMPORARY)

A restaurant kitchen. Stations: grill, cold, pastry, pass. High cohesion means everything at the grill station is about grilling. Low coupling means the pastry chef does not reach into the grill chef's drawer for a spoon; they ask at the pass. When a new person joins, you say "you own cold" and that sentence means something, because the station has edges. The day someone starts storing the desserts in the grill fridge because it was closer, nothing breaks. It just gets slightly harder to know where anything is, forever.

Files get split for the same reason rooms have walls: so that two people can work without standing on each other, and so that you can hold one piece in your head at a time. A file you cannot read in one sitting is a file nobody fully knows, which means every change to it is partly a guess.

Frameworks exist because most apps need the same boring machinery: routing, drawing, data flow, navigation. A framework hands you that machinery plus a set of opinions about where things live. You pay in freedom and in versions to upgrade. You get back the thing you cannot buy any other way, which is that a new person, or a new agent, already knows the shape of your app before they open it.

The habit underneath all of this is smaller than it sounds: write the trade-off down. Every structural choice costs something. An engineer who says "this is better" has not finished. An engineer who says "this is better, and here is what it costs us, and here is when that bill arrives" has.

ANY APP

The no-build version is the oldest arrangement on the web and it is still the fastest to work in. Files load top to bottom, everything can see everything, and a change is live the moment you refresh. The price is that nothing declares what it needs, so the correct order exists only in one HTML file and in people's heads.

The typed version inverts every one of those. Slower to set up, safer to change, and the compiler holds the map so no human has to.

Technical debt, with your own specimens

Technical debt is a loan. You take it when you ship something faster than the clean version, and you pay interest on every future change that touches it. Like a real loan it is not a moral failure and it is often the right call. The failure mode is the unrecorded loan: the one nobody decided to take, that nobody can find, that shows up as "why is this taking three days".

Memento's debt is unusual in one specific way: most of it is self-documented. The comments say what happened and why. That is the difference between a loan and a mess. Here is the survey, verified at the pin, stated as numbers.

Chapter 4 measured the mechanism at scale so you do not have to take my word for it: GitClear's study of 211 million changed lines found duplicated blocks up eight times in 2024, with copy-paste passing refactoring for the first time on record, and DORA found AI raises delivery speed and instability together unless testing and version control are strong. Agents make writing cheap. Nothing about them makes deciding where a thing belongs cheap. So the natural outcome of fast agent work is more code, more of it duplicated, and a structure that drifts from whatever the documentation says. Every specimen in the table above is that mechanism, not a character flaw.

The antidotes you already run

The interesting part of this chapter is that you built the countermeasures before you had the vocabulary for them.

  • The freeze. MIGRATION/FROZEN-AT reads version: v1378 / commit: ca8afc8 / frozen: 2026-09-11, and CLAUDE.md:52-53 makes it law: the web app is frozen, bug fixes only. A frozen system stops accruing new debt while its replacement is built. That is the single highest-leverage move available to someone rewriting an app.
  • The log. MIGRATION/FREEZE-LOG.md asks for one line per fix: version, file, what changed, why it could not wait, approved by. It currently reads "(none yet)", which is the strongest possible evidence the freeze is holding.
  • One owner per file. The migration plan assigns folders to Fable or Codex and insists on one owner at a time for manifests, config, router, state and auth. That is a boundary enforced socially instead of by a compiler, and it works for the same reason boundaries in code work.
  • The review gate. "APPROVED @ <sha>" pins an approval to an exact commit, so approval cannot drift onto later work nobody read.
  • Grep before you build. The habit that prevents the second system from being born at all. Two minutes of searching beats a year of two paywalls.

And the business version, which is one sentence: debt is a loan, so take it on purpose, at a size you chose, and write the row down where the next person will look. The model for that row is already in your repo. The shim's comment block is longer than its code, and it tells whoever finds it what happened, why, and what would break without it. That is what a well-taken loan looks like.

THE ALTERNATIVE, AND WHY MEMENTO CHOSE THIS

The web app could have adopted a bundler and modules at any point, which would turn load order into declared imports and make a wrong order a build error instead of a crash on someone's phone. It would also add a build step between editing a file and seeing it, and a class of tooling failures that plain script tags simply do not have, on a project where one person ships several times a day. Memento chose speed of iteration and paid with the numbered filenames, the hand-written ?v= cache busting, and 29 test scripts listed by hand. The rebuild makes the opposite trade deliberately, which is the honest way to change an architecture: not by fixing the old one in place, but by starting a new one with the old one frozen beside it.

Model answer

Where: not in a screen component. Streak counting is a rule, and the native app already has a home for rules, src/rules/, which holds logic ported from the web deliberately; a screen should ask for the number, not know how it is produced.

Couples to: the shape of the day records, the date handling, and, invisibly, the web app's version of the same rule, because step 2 copies it. Copying creates a coupling with no line connecting the two files, which is the worst kind: it binds them without telling anyone.

Debt: a second implementation of one rule. The interest is paid the first time the rule changes, because a fix to one side produces two apps showing a user two different streaks, exactly like the second sandbox above. Step 6 removes the one thing that would have caught it. The cheaper version: move the rule into src/rules/ once, have both screens call it, and if the port must be a copy, write the row in the ledger saying it is a copy of a named file and what happens when they drift.

WORDS YOU NOW OWN
Coupling
How much one piece has to know about another's insides. High coupling means a change over here forces a change over there.
Cohesion
How much a file is about one thing. A 10,478-line file that holds four unrelated jobs has low cohesion.
Boundary
A promise between two pieces: this is what I guarantee, the rest is mine, do not reach past it. An interface instead of a URL.
Import graph
The machine-readable map of which file needs which. The web app has none, so its map is the load order in one HTML file.
Technical debt
A loan taken to move faster. Interest is paid on every future change that touches the shortcut. Fine when recorded, expensive when nobody decided to take it.
Shim
A small forwarder that keeps an old name working after the thing behind it moved or died. Debt, done in the open.
Refactor
Changing the shape of code without changing what it does. This is how debt gets repaid, and why a test suite is what makes repayment affordable.

The habit that separates a decision from a drift. Name the cost and when the bill arrives, in the place the next person will look.
WHERE TODAY'S ANALOGY BREAKS

Breaks when: you picture stations that stay put. A kitchen's layout is physical and changing it takes a weekend; a codebase's layout is rearranged by every commit, including ones nobody thought of as structural. The ledger picture also suggests debt is a list of known items, and the expensive ones are almost always the items nobody wrote down, because they were never decided. Real model: architecture is not a diagram you draw once, it is the accumulated result of thousands of small "where should this go" answers, most of them given in a hurry, now increasingly given by agents that do not carry your intent between sessions. The countermeasure is not a better diagram. It is the freeze, the owner, the gate, and the row in the log, which is the system you already run.

RABBIT HOLE: where the term came from

Ward Cunningham coined "technical debt" in 1992 to describe shipping code that is not quite right about the problem, on purpose, to learn faster, as long as you go back and refactor once you understand the problem better. The part that survived is "debt". The part usually dropped is the second half: the loan is only worth taking if the repayment is real. Martin Fowler later split it four ways: reckless or prudent, deliberate or inadvertent. Prudent-deliberate is the shim in chapter 1. Prudent-inadvertent is the one that matters most to a founder: you did it the best way you knew, then learned more, and now the old shape is wrong. That one is not avoidable by being smarter up front. It is only manageable by noticing.

RABBIT HOLE: why "just rewrite it" is usually wrong, and when it is not

The standard warning is that a rewrite throws away years of accumulated fixes, most of which look like ugly lines that are actually a bug someone hit at 2am. The counter-case is when the constraints themselves changed, so the old code is not wrong so much as pointed at a different target. Memento's rebuild is the second kind: the web app was built for a browser and the goal is now an App Store binary on two platforms, which is not a refactor, it is a different machine. Notice the two things that make it survivable. The old app is frozen rather than abandoned, so it keeps serving users while the new one grows. And __tests__/ai-transport.test.ts runs the original code and compares outputs against the TypeScript version, which makes the old app the oracle for the new one. That is how you rewrite without re-losing every fix.
